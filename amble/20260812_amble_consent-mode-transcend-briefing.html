1. Why this is different for Amble

Amble sells compounded prescription medication (including GLP-1). Marketing and intake pages often name drugs in the URL and title — e.g. /glp-1-injections. That turns ordinary ad-tech behaviour into a health-data disclosure problem.

Three pressures stack:

  • State consumer-health / privacy laws (especially Washington MHMDA, Maryland MODPA, Nevada SB 370, plus sensitive-data opt-in states).
  • Federal enforcement (FTC telehealth/pixel cases — GoodRx, BetterHelp, Cerebral, Monument, and the July 2026 Hims & Hers suit).
  • Ad-platform contracts (Google/Meta terms that ban health-derived events and audiences regardless of user consent or US state).

The measurement question is therefore not only “how do we keep Google Ads numbers?” It is “what can we lawfully and contractually send when someone refuses Advertising — or never consented — on a drug-named page?”

2. Vocabulary (read this before the rest)

Opt-in vs opt-out (industry meaning — easy to reverse)

TermMeaningTracking at first load
Opt-in (default) Visitor must turn tracking on Off until they consent
Opt-out (default) Visitor is tracked until they turn it off On by default

If someone says “opt-in default” meaning “consented by default,” they usually mean what this document calls opt-out.

Two different products people confuse

PieceWhat it isVisible?
Transcend / CMP banner The preference UI (banner, modal, “manage settings”). Regional Experiences set per-state defaults. Yes — the popup
Google Consent Mode Invisible code that tells Google tags whether Advertising/Analytics are granted or denied, and (Basic vs Advanced) whether tags may load / send pings. No — background plumbing

Consent Mode is always installed site-wide. In an opt-out region it often sits in the “open” position (Advertising already granted) until someone closes it. That is not the same as turning Consent Mode off.

GPC — Global Privacy Control

GPC is a browser setting (or extension) that tells sites: treat me as opted out of sale/sharing / advertising tracking. The visitor does not have to click your banner. In the Transcend project language this is a Privacy Signal: it only ever lowers consent, and it beats a stored preference. Amble’s design also recognises Do Not Track (DNT).

Some states require honouring such a signal as a valid universal opt-out. The project Floor honours GPC/DNT in every Tier, not only the mandating states.

4. Why Consent Mode Advanced is a bad fit

Zak’s research (summarised) is the case against Advanced for Amble:

  1. What Advanced is supposed to buy: when a visitor refuses advertising, tags still send Google “cookieless pings” so Google can estimate conversions it cannot see. Sales pitch: refuse consent, keep numbers.
  2. What it actually sends (observed on the wire): under total refusal, requests still go to Google carrying the full page URL, referrer, and on conversion paths the purchase value, currency, conversion name, page address, and browser details. Documentation understates this; measurement is the reliable source.
  3. Why Amble is worse than a normal advertiser: URLs name drugs. A refuse still tells Google: this person bought, for $X, from the GLP-1 page. That pattern matches FTC telehealth enforcement and Washington-style health-data collection theories — and breaks Google’s own health-data contract.
  4. Redaction failed: replacing page title is possible; replacing the page address before it leaves is not (in the tests run). The part that names the drug is the part you cannot change.
  5. “Advanced only in relaxed states” fails twice: (a) Transcend cannot set Basic/Advanced per state via the normal Data Flow tag; (b) even if a workaround worked, Google’s contract does not change at a state line.
  6. What you actually give up by saying no to Advanced: Basic is not “no modelling.” Google still has a general model across advertisers. Advanced is a modest accuracy upgrade bought by sending drug-page traffic from people who said no.

5. Why “no identity attached” does not fix it

An instinctive defence — “once the session is not tied to identity, we should be fine” — was already made and retracted in this project’s classification research. Zak’s rebuttal, in short:

  1. The law does not require a name. Washington uses “linked or reasonably linkable.” IP address and cookie IDs clear that bar. Google receives IP, UA, and rich browser context, and often can attach signed-in Google accounts.
  2. Amble’s advertiser identity can supply the health fact. A stripped purchase still says “someone bought prescription medication” when the sender is a telehealth pharmacy brand. The sender is part of the disclosure.
  3. The FTC rejected the generic-event version of this argument (Monument: events reduced to “Sign Up” / “Paid” still “effectively disclosed” treatment signup).
  4. The live stack is not anonymous anyway (person profiles, email identifiers, remarketing audience endpoints on drug pages, etc.).
  5. Platform contracts ban health-related advertising data as a warranty — consent and anonymity do not rewrite the warranty.

Caveat Zak flagged for counsel: whether advertiser identity alone is enough under Washington is an open risk question — about residual risk size, not about anonymity making the risk disappear. A De-Healthed Event is “the least health-revealing form,” not a de-identification.

6. Basic vs Advanced Consent Mode

BasicAdvanced
When Advertising is on / granted Tags load → real conversion signals Same: real conversion signals
When Advertising is off / denied Tags do not load → no hit from that person Tags still load → cookieless pings
Modelling if denied At best Google’s general multi-advertiser model Advertiser-specific model trained partly on your denied traffic

Basic is a reasonable choice for Amble’s Google Ads setup precisely because Advanced’s denied-state pings are the clearest legal/contract exposure on Health-Revealing Pages.

Important: Consent Mode is not an alternative to “normal” conversion tracking. When Advertising is granted, you still get normal Google conversion tracking. Consent Mode is the layer that allows or blocks that behaviour.

7. What Transcend can vary by state (and what it cannot)

Transcend’s point is per-state experiences. That works for the banner / Purpose defaults. It does not work for Basic vs Advanced.

Can vary by Regional Experience (state)Cannot vary by state today (global)
Banner / UI Basic vs Advanced Consent Mode
Which Purposes apply Google Data Flow Purpose tags (Essential = Advanced; Advertising/Analytics = Basic)
Opt-in vs opt-out defaults per Purpose Built-in Meta LDU geography (needs custom wiring)
GPC / privacy-signal handling (Purpose-level) Server-side enforcement in Stape for non-Google tags (must gate yourself)

So: Basic (or Advanced) is one site-wide choice. You still use Regional Experiences so litigious/strict states can start Advertising off and relaxed states can differ on Analytics — but you do not get “Advanced in Texas, Basic in Washington” from the dashboard alone.

A custom workaround (block Google tag load by airgap.getRegimes() in strict regimes) is unproven and still leaves Advanced’s contract problem in relaxed states.

8. How conversion tracking would actually work

Assume: Basic Consent Mode nationally + Regional Experiences for Purpose defaults.

VisitorWhat Google gets under Basic
Opt-out-style region, Advertising still on Real conversion signals (Consent Mode reports granted)
Opt-out-style region, user opted out or GPC cleared Advertising Nothing (maybe weak general modelling)
Opt-in region, never consents Nothing (maybe weak general modelling)
Opt-in region, affirmatively opts in Real conversion signals

Correct analysis:

  • Consent Mode stays on in all states.
  • In regions where Advertising starts granted, Basic does not block — you get normal conversion tracking for people who remain on.
  • For people with Advertising off, Basic means no conversion hit; any estimate is general modelling, not Advanced pings from your site.

Incorrect analysis to avoid:

  • “Basic means Google models when ads are off” → that is closer to Advanced.
  • “In non-litigious states Consent Mode does not need to be used” → Consent Mode is still used; it just sits open while Advertising is granted.

9. Which states / the National Floor

A common plan is: opt-in in litigious states, opt-out in non-litigious states. For Advertising, the settled Transcend project design is stricter.

ADR 0001 (accepted): A National Floor applies to every visitor and never consults geolocation. Advertising and Sale Of Info are opt-in in every US state as part of that Floor. Only Analytics and Functional vary by State Tier. Tiers may add choice or notice on top of the Floor; they may never lower it.

Why national opt-in on Advertising: IP state detection is unreliable; Washington’s definition of “consumer” is not a clean residency test; a wrong “opt-out” guess under-protects. The Floor also already bars health-derived event names/paths/titles/audiences to ad platforms.

Four State Tiers (project map)

TierStatesAdvertisingAnalytics / Functional
Strict Health WA, NV (+ non-US / unresolved region) opt-in opt-in
Opt-In CT, CO, VA, MT, TX, OR, DE, NH, NJ, MN, TN, IN, KY, RI, NE opt-in opt-in
No consent path MD (sensitive-data sale banned; “strictly necessary” limits) effectively off / notice model off
Opt-Out CA, UT, IA + states with no comprehensive privacy law still opt-in under the Floor on by default

So: if the question is “where can Advertising start on?” under the ADRs — nowhere. If the question is “where can first-party Analytics start on?” — the Opt-Out Tier.

Extra Floor item for Washington: because MHMDA’s sale prohibition has no consent cure, ticket 07 added that no ad platform receives any event from a Washington visitor at any consent state (mitigated with declared checkout state as a second suppress signal).

10. Federal layer vs state opt-out

Is it “safe” to use Advertising opt-out in non-litigious states? Not as a green light for ad pixels / health-adjacent conversions.

  • State law: many non-strict states allow opt-out for ordinary sale/share. That does not erase health-sensitive theories where they apply, and it does not rewrite platform contracts.
  • Federal FTC: peer telehealth cases treat disclosure of health information to advertising platforms as the core unfair/deceptive practice — often remedied with a ban on advertising disclosures, not “you may send it if the banner was opt-out.” Hims (2026) is FTC-led with state partners.
  • Contracts: Google/Meta health restrictions apply in every US state.

That federal + contract layer is exactly why the project put Advertising on the National Floor instead of trusting “opt-out is fine in CA/TX/etc.”

11. Working decision summary

  1. Use Basic Consent Mode nationally for Google — Advanced’s denied-state pings on drug URLs are the worst exposure for the modest modelling gain.
  2. Keep Consent Mode installed everywhere. When Advertising is granted, normal conversion tracking still works.
  3. Use Transcend Regional Experiences for per-state UI and Purpose defaults — especially Analytics opt-out where lawful.
  4. Do not expect Transcend to give per-state Basic/Advanced via Data Flow tags alone.
  5. Follow the National Floor for Advertising: opt-in everywhere; never send health-derived URLs/event names/audiences to ad platforms; Washington gets an ad blackout regardless of consent.
  6. De-identification / “no user id” is not the escape hatch for health-ad disclosures; counsel owns residual risk on advertiser-identity theories.
  7. Session replay is a separate workstream (PostHog/Sentry) from this Consent Mode decision — still high risk, different case pattern.
One-line mental model: Transcend decides whether Advertising starts on or off by state (within the Floor). Consent Mode Basic decides what Google gets when Advertising is off (nothing). Platform + FTC rules decide what you may put in a granted conversion even when Advertising is on.
